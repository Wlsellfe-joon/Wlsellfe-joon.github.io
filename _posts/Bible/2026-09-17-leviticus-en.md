Leviticus is less a fast-moving historical narrative than a system for ordering sacrifice, purity, ethics, festivals, and land under one central premise: a holy deity dwells among the community. The modern question “Is this law scientifically correct?” therefore cannot, by itself, explain what the book is trying to do. “Clean” and “unclean” in Leviticus are not synonyms for hygienic and unhygienic, or morally good and evil. Childbirth, menstruation, and sexual intercourse—ordinary processes that are not sins—can create temporary ritual impurity.

This article respects the book as a confession of faith but does not ask philology, archaeology, or medicine to prove more than their methods can establish. Finds showing that people in ancient Judah burned incense and sacrificed animals illuminate the book's cultural setting. They do not prove that God dictated a particular rule to Moses at Mount Sinai. Likewise, a rule may have an incidental hygienic effect without having been based on germ theory.

> **Evidence scale**
>
> - **Directly relevant:** A manuscript, inscription, artifact, or measured residue directly preserves the text or practice under discussion.
> - **Contextually consistent:** Real evidence resembles the setting, institution, or custom in the book but does not verify the rule's origin or the narrated event.
> - **Disputed:** Specialists disagree about the date, causal connection, ethnic attribution, or relationship to the text.
> - **Insufficient evidence:** Available evidence cannot establish the claim.
> - **Not testable / matter of faith:** Divine speech, supernatural judgment, and the spiritual efficacy of atonement cannot be adjudicated by historical science.

An Oxford Bibliographies overview treats Leviticus not as an isolated event record but as part of the Pentateuch's priestly source. It assigns most of chapters 1–16 to P and the Holiness Code in chapters 17–26, chapter 27, and several additions to H. A prominent recent model understands H as revising, supplementing, and completing P.[^1]

## 2. Authorship

### The traditional view: Moses

Jewish and Christian tradition has long attributed Leviticus, as part of the Torah, to Moses. “The LORD spoke to Moses” recurs throughout the book, and its final verse frames the laws as commandments delivered to Israel through Moses at Sinai (Lev 27:34). In this reading Moses is both author and mediator of revelation, teaching Aaron and the priests how to perform the rites.

The book does not, however, explicitly say that Moses personally wrote all twenty-seven chapters. The narrator speaks about Moses in the third person and arranges commands, legal cases, short narratives, and an appendix. The observation that tradition anchors the book's authority in Moses is therefore not identical to the historical proposition that one person wrote every sentence of the present Hebrew text at one time.

### Modern literary analysis: P, H, and editing

Modern scholarship compares vocabulary, recurring formulas, views of sanctuary and priesthood, and relationships with Deuteronomy, Ezekiel, and other legal texts. There is broad agreement that Leviticus is priestly literature, but no agreement on every layer or its absolute date.

- **P (Priestly material):** Generally includes the sacrificial, ordination, and purity system in most of chapters 1–16.
- **H (Holiness material):** Centers on chapters 17–26 and extends “Be holy” to the ethics, agriculture, sexuality, and calendar of the entire people. Chapter 27 and smaller edits earlier in the book are also often associated with H.
- **Their relationship:** Older models sometimes placed H before P. An influential current view holds that H knew and supplemented P, although the allocation and date of individual verses remain disputed.[^1][^2]

Literary analysis cannot prove that Moses never existed. Conversely, traditional attribution cannot by itself establish Mosaic autograph. The cautious conclusion is: **Moses is the traditional author and authoritative mediator, while the surviving text is analyzed as a composite work preserved and shaped by anonymous priestly scribes and editors.**

## 3. Date and Composition

### The story's internal date

Exodus 40:17 dates the tabernacle's erection to the first day of the first month in the second year after the exodus. Numbers 1:1 begins the next census on the first day of the second month. In the Pentateuch's present sequence, Leviticus occupies roughly the month between those markers. That is an **internal narrative chronology**, not external evidence that the book was written during that month.

### The age of a rite is not the date of a text

Sacrifice, handling blood, purification periods, festivals, and fallow years may preserve customs older than their written formulation. Conversely, an older custom can be redescribed to fit a later centralized sanctuary and priesthood. A legal text can report practice, attempt to reform it, or present an ideal that was never fully implemented.

Scholarly dates range from models stressing pre-exilic or late-monarchic material to models placing P and H in the Babylonian exile and Persian period. Many critical reconstructions place the completion of the present form around the sixth or fifth centuries BCE, while other scholars argue that language and institutions preserve earlier cores. “Written in the fifth century BCE” is therefore not an experimentally fixed date. A more accurate conclusion is that **traditions from multiple periods underwent literary work across the late monarchy, exile, and Persian period; the exact stages and dates remain disputed**.[^2]

### What the surviving manuscripts establish

Hebrew and Greek fragments of Leviticus were recovered at Qumran. They directly show that, from several centuries BCE into the first century CE, the book circulated in more than one script and language. Comparison of the Masoretic Text, Samaritan Pentateuch, Septuagint, and Dead Sea Scrolls suggests fewer large-scale differences in Leviticus than in some biblical books, but small additions, omissions, arrangements, spellings, and translation differences remain real.[^16][^18]

A carbonized scroll found in the synagogue at En-Gedi was read with micro-CT scanning and “virtual unwrapping.” It preserves portions of Leviticus 1–2. Radiocarbon evidence points mainly to the third or fourth century CE, while the relationship between that date and paleographic proposals has been reassessed. Within the recovered passage, the consonantal text agrees with the later Masoretic tradition. This is direct evidence for textual transmission, not a Mosaic autograph or proof that the narrated rites occurred at Sinai.[^17][^18]

## 4. Geographic and Historical Setting

### Literary setting: the portable sanctuary at Sinai

Nearly every speech in Leviticus centers on the tabernacle at the foot of Mount Sinai. Exodus ends with the sanctuary completed but inaccessible even to Moses because of the divine glory. Leviticus answers the resulting question: who may approach holy space, in what condition, and through what procedure? The camp is imagined concentrically—the sanctuary at the center, priests and Israel around it, and the wilderness outside.

Neither the exact location of biblical Mount Sinai nor artifacts securely identified as this tabernacle have been found. A tent complex of textiles, skins, and wood would preserve poorly, but low preservation potential does not turn a missing object into a discovered one. Claims that a particular structure is “Moses' tabernacle” remain supported by **insufficient evidence**.

### Historical setting: sanctuaries and priestly societies in Israel and Judah

The social worlds behind the text may include multiple Iron Age sanctuaries in Israel and Judah, the fall of the kingdoms and Babylonian exile, and the Jerusalem temple community in Persian-period Yehud. Archaeology has recovered altars, sanctuary architecture, and animal remains at sites such as Arad, Dan, and Moza. These finds provide context for real sacrifice and ritual meals; they do not show that every local rite matched Leviticus.[^4][^5]

Leviticus 17 requires slaughtered sacrificial animals to be brought to the tent of meeting. Chapters 17–26 extend priestly holiness into communal sexual, economic, and agricultural conduct. Which period of centralization and boundary-making these rules reflect remains disputed. A normative document can mirror practice, try to change it, or describe an ideal rarely observed.

### Modern geography

Proposed Sinai locations lie in and around today's Egyptian Sinai Peninsula, but none commands a scholarly consensus. The principal comparative material comes from the southern Levant, encompassing present-day Israel, Palestine, and Jordan. Modern borders and identities should not be projected directly onto ancient names.

## 6. Detailed Summary

### 6.1 Five offerings and priestly portions (chapters 1–7)

Leviticus opens with the voluntary burnt offering. The offerer places a hand on the animal and slaughters it; the priest handles the blood and altar. The burnt offering (*olah*) is almost wholly burned. The grain offering (*minhah*) uses fine flour, oil, and frankincense. In the well-being offering (*zevah shelamim*), altar portions are burned while priests and offerers share the remainder in a ritual meal.

The *hatta't*, traditionally “sin offering,” also appears in purification after childbirth and uses blood to decontaminate sacred space; “purification offering” is therefore a prominent scholarly translation. The *asham* is a reparation offering, especially when misuse of sacred property or property damage requires restitution plus twenty percent. Levitical sacrifice is not one undifferentiated mechanism in which every animal receives guilt. Gift, homage, shared meal, purification, and reparation are distinct functions.[^3]

Leviticus 1:1–6:7 addresses the offerer; 6:8–7:38 adds priestly handling, time and place of eating, and prohibitions on consuming blood and certain fat. Some offerings scale down from livestock to birds or flour for people with fewer means. That accommodation should be noted, but so should the system's dependence on animal killing and priestly dues.

### 6.2 Ordination, inaugural worship, and the deaths of Nadab and Abihu (chapters 8–10)

Moses washes Aaron and his four sons, clothes them, and applies oil and blood to distinguish priests and sanctuary. After seven days of ordination, the first public service takes place on the eighth day, and fire from Yahweh consumes the offering. Nadab and Abihu then present “unauthorized fire” and are killed by fire from before Yahweh.

The text does not fully explain their offense. The nearby ban on priestly drinking and the command to distinguish holy from common and clean from unclean emphasize the danger and responsibility of priestly judgment. The story makes a theological claim about obedience near holy space; fire from God is not a cause historical science can test.

### 6.3 Edible animals, bodies, surfaces, and discharges (chapters 11–15)

Chapter 11 classifies land animals by hoof and rumination and aquatic animals by fins and scales, then regulates contact with carcasses. Chapter 12 gives periods of impurity after childbirth. Chapters 13–14 address *tzaraat* on human skin and hair, textiles, and house walls. Chapter 15 covers ordinary and abnormal genital discharges.

“Unclean” here does not mean morally guilty or a person of dirty character. Childbirth, menstruation, and seminal emission are normal processes that nevertheless restrict access to the sanctuary for a time. Washing, waiting, inspection, and isolation can have practical side effects, but the system's stated goal is management of sacred boundaries, not microbial control.[^9]

### 6.4 The Day of Atonement: resetting sanctuary and community (chapter 16)

Aaron screens the inner sanctuary with incense smoke and prepares a bull and two goats. One goat is killed as Yahweh's purification offering; its blood decontaminates inner sanctuary, tent, and altar. Israel's offenses are confessed over the living goat, which carries them into the wilderness “for Azazel.” The two goats perform complementary movements: internal purification by blood and removal of pollution outside the camp.

The modern social metaphor “scapegoat” derives from this rite, but the second goat is not an innocent person irrationally blamed by a crowd. It is a ritual carrier of confessed communal offenses. Whether Azazel is a wilderness being, a place, or an expression of complete removal cannot be established with certainty.

### 6.5 Blood, sexuality, neighbor, and resident alien (chapters 17–20)

Chapter 17 identifies blood with life, forbids consuming it, and links sacrifice with the tent of meeting. Chapters 18 and 20 prohibit incestuous relations, adultery, male-male intercourse, bestiality, and child sacrifice, assigning capital punishment to some acts. Directly equating these rules with modern concepts of consent, sexual orientation, human rights, and criminal law erases substantial historical differences.

Chapter 19 places ritual and food rules beside respect for parents; gleanings for the poor and resident alien; bans on theft, deception, withheld wages, and exploitation of disability; impartial courts; and honest measures. “Love your neighbor as yourself” (19:18) is extended to the resident alien (19:34). Holiness in Leviticus therefore includes economy, speech, courts, and treatment of outsiders, not ritual alone.[^14]

### 6.6 Priestly bodies and sacred food (chapters 21–22)

Priests face stricter rules concerning corpses, marriage, hair, and bodily marks. Chapter 21 permits descendants of Aaron with specified physical differences to eat sacred portions but bars them from altar service. Sacrificial animals are likewise expected to be without prescribed blemishes.

The rule shows how an ancient sanctuary represented wholeness and boundaries through bodies. It is not a scientific measure of the capability or dignity of disabled people. Disability scholarship acknowledges the text's real exclusion while warning later communities not to turn it into authorization for discrimination.[^15]

### 6.7 Festivals, sabbatical years, and Jubilee (chapters 23–25)

Chapter 23 orders the year through Sabbath, Passover and Unleavened Bread, first produce, Weeks, Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and Booths. Chapter 24 covers lamps and bread, narrates a blasphemy case, and gives proportional injury and property rules.

Chapter 25 orders the land to rest every seventh year and proclaims Jubilee after seven sabbatical cycles. Family land and the status of Israelite debt servants are to be restored because land ultimately belongs to God. This is a radical limit on inherited poverty, but foreigners may still be bought, held, and inherited as permanent slaves (25:44–46). Presenting Jubilee as either universal modern human rights or abolition of slavery would sanitize the text.[^12]

### 6.8 Blessings, curses, and valuations (chapters 26–27)

Chapter 26 promises rain, harvest, peace, and divine presence for obedience, then escalates disease, war, famine, and exile for rebellion. It is dangerous to turn this covenantal theology into a universal formula blaming every disaster on its victims. The passage presents theological causation, not a natural-scientific model of catastrophe.

Chapter 27 sets valuations for redeeming vows concerning people, animals, houses, and fields, along with rules for firstborn things, devoted property, and tithes. The appendix shows that sacred dedication also required accounting and economic procedure.
## 7. Scientific, Archaeological, and Historical Evidence Review

### 7.1 The result first: what has and has not been found

Archaeology and manuscripts directly establish Iron Age altars and sanctuaries in Israel and Judah; frankincense and cannabis residues on eighth-century BCE Arad altars; regional differences in pig consumption; and actual copies of Leviticus among the Qumran fragments and the later En-Gedi scroll.

They do not provide a contemporary inscription saying that Moses or Aaron wrote and enforced Leviticus, an authenticated Sinai tabernacle, an independent account of Nadab and Abihu or the wilderness Day of Atonement, or administrative proof that all ancient Israelites uniformly observed all twenty-seven chapters at one point in time.

Law and lived practice are not identical. A prohibition does not prove the prohibited practice never occurred. An artifact resembling a biblical practice does not prove that a particular verse caused it.

### 7.2 Frankincense and cannabis at Arad: chemical evidence of real ritual

Dark residues on two limestone altars from the Judahite shrine at Tel Arad were analyzed independently in two laboratories. The small altar yielded THC, CBD, CBN, and terpenes together with animal-dung residues, suggesting cannabis flower or resin was heated mildly. The large altar yielded boswellic acid, norursatriene, and animal-fat compounds, indicating frankincense resin evaporated with fat. This is direct chemical evidence for incense ritual in eighth-century BCE Judah.[^6]

Leviticus includes frankincense with grain offerings and the bread of the Presence, so its use is **contextually consistent**. It does not prescribe cannabis as incense. The finding may instead show that local ritual combinations did not perfectly duplicate the literary rules. Arad is not the Sinai tabernacle and dates centuries after the narrative setting.

**Assessment:** **Direct evidence** for eighth-century Judahite incense ritual; **not direct evidence** for the origin of Leviticus or a Sinai ceremony.

### 7.3 Sacrificial animal bones: how far can they take us?

Zooarchaeology combines species, age, sex, body-part distribution, cut and burn marks, and excavation context. Sheep, goat, and cattle bones with slaughter, dining, altar, and sanctuary evidence at sites such as Dan and Moza permit study of real sacrifice and ritual feasting. These species overlap with the principal Levitical offerings.[^4][^5]

Yet sheep, goats, and cattle also dominate ordinary diets. A few bones cannot tell us that a meal was specifically a burnt or well-being offering, or that an Aaronide priest manipulated blood in the order required by Leviticus. Architecture, tools, selected body parts, demographic profile, and processing traces must converge before the ritual inference becomes strong. Researchers caution against using the Bible as a checklist that automatically labels excavated bones “Yahwistic sacrifice.”[^5]

**Assessment:** Animal sacrifice and sanctuary meals are **directly attested general practices**. Exact compliance with Leviticus 1–7 is only **partial and contextual correspondence**.

### 7.4 Was sacrifice an Israelite invention?

Offering animals, grain, and incense to deities, with portions consumed by priests or worshipers, was widespread across the ancient Near East and Mediterranean. Leviticus works within that shared ritual language while associating blood with life, organizing offerings under one deity and sanctuary, and distinguishing error, impurity, and reparation in detail. Comparison establishes common culture and distinctive choices; direct literary borrowing requires more specific evidence.[^4]

Archaeology can investigate where victims were raised, how they lived, how they were killed, and how remains were distributed. It cannot measure whether an aroma pleased God or blood actually atoned for sin.

**Assessment:** The ancient cultural setting of sacrifice is **strongly contextually supported**. Spiritual atonement is **not testable and belongs to faith**.

### 7.5 Pig bones and dietary law: evidence of identity, but not a simple proof

Leviticus 11 excludes the pig because it has a divided hoof but does not ruminate. Faunal studies in the southern Levant find very low proportions of pig bones at many Iron Age highland and Judahite sites. Philistine cities and some lowland sites in the northern kingdom had higher pork consumption at particular times. Researchers have proposed a relationship between increased pork consumption in the northern lowlands during Iron IIB and the consolidation of a pig taboo in late-monarchic Judah.[^7]

The syllogism “few pig bones = observance of Leviticus 11 = Israelite inhabitants” does not follow. Pig husbandry depends on water, shade, feed, and settlement strategy, and non-Israelite groups also avoided pigs. Bones document consumption, not the reasons people gave for it.

Parasite risk in poorly prepared pork does not prove that the taboo began as ancient public health. Permitted meats can also transmit disease when mishandled, and Leviticus explicitly grounds classification in holiness and separation rather than a medical rationale. Ecology, economy, symbolic classification, and group identity are more adequate together than a single hygiene theory.[^7][^8]

**Assessment:** Pork avoidance in parts of Israel and Judah is **zooarchaeologically supported**. Direct enforcement of Leviticus and a single hygienic purpose are **not established**.

### 7.6 “Unclean” is not “infected,” and *tzaraat* is not Hansen's disease

Ritual impurity arises from non-sinful conditions such as childbirth, menstruation, semen, and contact with a corpse, and it ends after time, washing, and sometimes ritual. Language of murder, idolatry, and certain sexual offenses defiling the land functions differently as moral impurity. Confusing these categories turns mothers and patients into sinners whom the text does not identify as such.[^9]

It is also inaccurate to equate *tzaraat* in Leviticus 13–14 with “leprosy” or modern Hansen's disease. The same word covers changes in skin and hair, textiles, and house walls. Dermatological studies conclude that the human descriptions group several kinds of lesions and are unlikely to correspond to one modern disease. Hansen's disease is a chronic infection involving *Mycobacterium leprae* or a related bacterium; the Levitical priest performs no bacterial test or therapy but observes, isolates, reexamines, and declares ritual status.[^10]

Seven-day review, laundering, and removal of affected material could incidentally reduce contact. But *tzaraat* of buildings and the inclusion of normal menstruation and semen in the same broad system show that this is not a modern infection-control code. Claims that Leviticus anticipated germ theory overstate both the text and medicine.

**Assessment:** Some practical effects of observation, washing, and isolation are **possible**. A modern diagnosis or germ-theory origin has **insufficient evidence**. The primary system is **ritual and theological**.

### 7.7 The goat for Azazel and ancient Near Eastern elimination rites

Hittite and Mesopotamian texts describe rites that transfer illness, curse, or pollution to an animal or object and dispatch it to an uninhabited place. David P. Wright compared such rites with the released bird in Leviticus 14 and the goat for Azazel in chapter 16.[^11] The similarities show that symbolically carrying pollution outside inhabited space was intelligible in ancient West Asia.

The deities, words, animals, and aims differ across the rites. A comparison does not demonstrate that a Levitical author read a particular Hittite tablet or simply copied the Day of Atonement. Azazel's identity also remains disputed.

**Assessment:** The elimination-rite pattern is **contextually consistent**. Direct borrowing and a Sinai origin are **unresolved**. The claim that sin was spiritually removed is a **matter of faith**.

### 7.8 Jubilee: ancient debt release and the question of implementation

Mesopotamian kings sometimes issued *mīšarum* or *andurārum* decrees releasing certain debts and debt servants. These texts directly attest the social problem of losing freedom and property through debt. Leviticus 25 turns episodic royal release into an ideal periodic restoration after seven cycles of seven years.

Outside Leviticus, however, the Hebrew Bible contains no unambiguous report of nationwide land restoration on a fifty-year schedule called Jubilee. Jeremiah 34 and Nehemiah 5 portray contested release of debt servants and restoration of property, not proof of a regular Jubilee cycle. No external archive demonstrates that land sales and manumission were institutionalized on that fifty-year timetable.[^12]

The Qumran text 11QMelchizedek combines Leviticus's Jubilee with Deuteronomy's seven-year debt release in an eschatological interpretation. That is direct evidence that Second Temple readers studied and reinterpreted Jubilee—not evidence that Iron Age Judah carried it out. Leviticus 25 itself does not explicitly cancel every debt, a gap that generated ancient legal debate.[^13]

**Assessment:** Debt crisis and ancient Near Eastern release decrees are **direct historical evidence**. Nationwide implementation of the Levitical fifty-year Jubilee has **insufficient evidence**.

### 7.9 Ethics: protections and ancient limits must both be visible

Leviticus 19 turns gleaning, same-day payment, fair weights, protection against exploiting disability, and love of neighbor and resident alien into matters of holiness. Extending love to the resident alien is a significant ethical strand that presses beyond a narrow kinship boundary.[^14]

The same corpus permits foreign people to be purchased and inherited as permanent slaves (25:44–46), prescribes death for certain sexual acts and blasphemy, gives different postpartum periods according to the sex of the child, and excludes priests with specified physical differences from altar service (21:16–23).[^15]

Even where a law compares favorably with another ancient code, that comparison does not make slavery, capital punishment, gender hierarchy, or disability exclusion right by modern human-rights standards. History can analyze the law's social location and reception. Whether it should govern people today is a separate ethical and theological judgment.[^12]

### 7.10 Qumran and En-Gedi: the most direct evidence for the text

The Leviticus evidence at Qumran includes Hebrew manuscripts and fragments of the Greek Septuagint. The witnesses contain spelling and smaller reading differences, demonstrating that Second Temple transmission was not simply copied from a single medieval manuscript. At the same time, the broad structure and substantial wording of Leviticus were already present.[^16]

A 2024 study of Qumran Leviticus manuscripts considers whether additions and omissions may sometimes reflect literary development rather than only copying errors. Not every variant represents a new edition; scribal habit, physical damage and reconstruction, and other ancient versions must be compared.[^18]

The En-Gedi scroll was a charred lump that would have disintegrated if physically opened. Researchers segmented its layers with X-ray tomography, computationally flattened the surfaces, and combined texture and ink signals. They recovered two columns from Leviticus 1–2, including eighteen complete and seventeen partial lines. It is a landmark case in which archaeology and computer science recovered an actual biblical manuscript.[^17]

**Assessment:** Circulation of Leviticus in the Second Temple period and late antiquity has **direct evidence**. The first author, first date of composition, and events at Sinai do **not**.

### 7.11 God, atonement, and judgment: the domain of faith

The following claims are central to Leviticus but cannot be adjudicated by experiment or excavation:

- God actually spoke to Moses from the tabernacle.
- Sacrificial blood and the Day of Atonement removed sin before God and cleansed sacred space.
- Divine fire consumed the inaugural offering and judged Nadab and Abihu.
- Obedience or rebellion supernaturally determined rain, disease, war, and exile.
- God dwelt among the people and required them to be holy because God is holy.

A believer may receive these as revelation and spiritual reality. A nonreligious reader may understand them as the theological language by which an ancient community organized order, guilt, and restoration. Archaeology can study traces of communities copying these claims and performing rites; it cannot measure whether a deity accepted them.

## 8. Conclusion

The strongest archaeological evidence relevant to Leviticus is not an artifact belonging to Moses or a Sinai tabernacle. It is the **material residue left by sacrifice and diet, together with ancient manuscripts containing the book itself**. Frankincense on the Arad altar, animal remains from sanctuaries, regional pig-consumption patterns, Qumran fragments, and the En-Gedi scroll are real data. They concern eighth-century and later ritual, long-term food practice, and textual circulation from the Second Temple period onward. They do not establish that every Levitical rule originated at Sinai.

The purity laws should not be repackaged as modern medicine. *Tzaraat* is not identical with Hansen's disease, and ritual impurity arises from normal childbirth, menstruation, and sexual activity. Washing, observation, and isolation may have produced incidental benefits, but Leviticus classifies the world theologically through sanctuary, life, death, and wholeness. Low pig-bone frequencies support food avoidance but cannot, alone, disclose its motive or date the law.

Ethically, Leviticus connects holiness to wages, impartial judgment, care for the poor and resident alien, and restoration of family land. It also contains permanent foreign slavery, capital punishment, gendered rules, and priestly exclusion based on disability. Religious and nonreligious readers alike should read both sides without concealment.

Whether God spoke, sacrifice truly atoned for sin, or a holy presence dwelt in the tabernacle lies in the **domain of faith**, beyond historical-scientific testing. Evidence-centered reading does not disguise theology as science, and it does not convert an untestable religious claim into established history merely because science cannot rule on it.
